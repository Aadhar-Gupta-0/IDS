07 · Business use + limitations

USE THE SCORE TO
PRIORITISE — NOT JUDGE.

01

Rank outreach

Recruiters can sort a large pool and start where model scores are highest.

02

Choose threshold by capacity

A lower threshold catches more potential movers; a higher threshold reduces calls.

03

Human review

Never turn this into an automatic hiring or rejection rule.

Data limitation

One programme and one historical sample. Intent is not the same thing as future behaviour.

Fairness limitation

City can proxy socioeconomic context; subgroup errors need an explicit audit before real use.

Performance ceiling

~0.80 ROC-AUC is useful ranking signal, not certainty. Better behavioural and longitudinal features are the next data win.

THE QUESTION

Given a trainee's education, experience, current employer and city, can we predict whether they are looking for a job change?

19,158labelled trainees in aug_train.csv
2,129new trainees in aug_test.csv to score
14input columns + target to work with

WHY NOT ACCURACY?

A model that says “nobody is leaving” scores 75%.

Only 24.9% of trainees are looking (4,777 of 19,158). So the lazy model that always predicts “not looking” is right three times out of four — and finds zero job hunters. It sits in a burning room saying this is fine.

75.1%Dummy accuracy
vs
0job hunters found

That is exactly why we score with ROC-AUC and F1, never accuracy.

ROC-AUC 0.81

Pick one person who is job hunting and one who is not: the model ranks the hunter higher 81% of the time. 0.50 is a coin flip.

Recall 78%

Of everyone who really is looking, we catch 78%. The other 22% are the ones we miss.

Precision 52%

Half the people we flag really are looking. Random calling hits 25%, so we are 2.1× better than guessing.

F1 0.63

The balance of precision and recall — one number to compare models when the classes are lopsided.

🎯

Business value in plain numbers: with 2 recruiters you can only call a slice of the list. Ranking 19,158 trainees by probability means the first 800 calls reach 78% of everyone who is actually looking instead of the 25% you would hit at random.

STOP 03 · PART 2 · DATA PREPARATION

The missing data was
hiding the job hunters

Four columns are missing between 15% and 32% of their values. The textbook move is to drop every incomplete row. We checked what that costs first — and changed our minds.

03

🗑️

What dropping the rows would do: delete 53% of the dataset and drag the job-change rate in what is left from 25% down to 17%. The job hunters are hiding in the missing rows. So we kept every row: missing categories became an explicit “Unknown” level, missing numbers got the median — fitted on training data only.

From messy text to 152 numbers

14

Raw inputs

Drop enrollee_id first — an ID predicts nothing. Turn experience (<1, >20) into real years (0.5 and 21) and last_new_job (never, >4) into years (0 and 5). Order kept, 28 dummy columns saved.

STOP 07 · PART 3 · WHAT DRIVES THE PREDICTIONS

Plot twist: it's mostly
about where you live

We shuffled one column at a time and watched how much the test ROC-AUC dropped. The bigger the drop, the more the model leans on that column. This is predictive importance, not cause.

07

58% vs 17%

job hunting in cities below 0.60 versus 0.90 and up. A target-rate difference in this sample, not a causal effect.

39% vs 18%

no employer on record versus working at a Pvt Ltd. Missing ≠ unemployed — it means nobody wrote it down.

0.0003

ROC-AUC lost when gender is dropped from the model. A sensitive feature: remove it and audit errors by group.

What raises the odds

  • company_type = Unknown — no employer recorded, one of the strongest positive coefficients
  • city_21 — the big low-development city (index 0.62, 59% job-change rate)
  • city_103, city_160, city_100 — individual city effects stacked on top of the index

What lowers the odds

  • major_discipline = Unknown — the largest negative coefficient (~20% job-change rate)
  • city_development_index — higher index, sharply lower odds (coefficient −0.61 per SD)
  • Funded Startup, more experience, relevant experience — senior people with options tend to stay

Training hours, gender and last_new_job add almost nothing: 0.0008, 0.0006 and 0.0008 ROC-AUC respectively. The number a training company would naturally brag about is dead last.

STOP 08 · PART 4 · CONCLUSIONS

Can HR use the model?
Only as decision support.

The final deployment ensemble reaches locked-test ROC-AUC 0.8036 and 80.19% accuracy at a validation-selected 0.43 threshold. That is useful ranking signal — not enough to decide for a person.

08

01

Research, don't automate

Use the scores to study aggregate patterns or plan follow-up research — never as a verdict on a person.

02

Validate before use

Choose thresholds with validation data, business costs and recruiter capacity, not on the test set.

03

Keep humans in the loop

Never automatically reject, rank or contact people from model predictions alone. A recruiter reviews every case.

IF HR DOES USE IT, THIS IS HOW

Rank, don't label

Use the score to decide who to call first — never as a yes/no verdict on a person.

Set the cut-off by capacity

0.5 when HR wants coverage, 0.7 when recruiter time is short.

Target where movers are

Trainees from less developed cities and people early in their careers.

PROOF IT RUNS

The same pipeline scored the unlabelled file: 2,129 new candidates ranked and saved to aug_test_predictions.csv in enrollee_id,target format. At threshold 0.5 it flags 36.7% of them. Every top-10 candidate lives in city_21 with no employer on record.

STOP 09 · PART 4 · LIMITATIONS & WAY FORWARD

What our model is bad at,
and what we'd do next

09

LIMITATIONS

  • Reported intent is not behaviour. The target is what people said, not actual resignation or future behaviour.
  • Associations are not causes. Missing, sampling, geographic and demographic bias may shape the patterns.
  • Weak generalisation. One programme, one point in time — results may not transfer to other organisations or years.
  • Not an employment decision rule. 1 in 2 flags is a false positive; it must never be the sole basis for a decision.

WAY FORWARD

  • Validate on new cohorts. Re-test performance, calibration and subgroup errors before any deployment.
  • Audit fairness and proxies. Compare errors across demographic and geographic groups; drop gender.
  • Choose thresholds responsibly. Calibration checks plus documented business requirements, on validation data.
  • Keep human review. Predictions are decision support only — retrain and track recall against what people actually did.
41%

New cohorts
